# Trip.com retention evidence for hybrid-policy review

## Evidence for the HR director

**Study design.** Bloom, Han and Liang's [2024 Nature study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07500-2) covered 1,612 university-graduate engineering, marketing and finance employees in Trip.com's Airfare and IT divisions in Shanghai. Interns and new employees on probation were excluded. The study took place at a single employer.

Assignment was random, based on whether an employee's birthday date was odd or even:

- 792 odd-birthday employees were allowed to work from home on Wednesday and Friday, leaving three office days. The two home days were optional.
- 820 even-birthday employees formed a control group with five office days.

The 518 initial volunteers started in the week of 9 August 2021. The remaining 1,094 non-volunteers started in the week of 13 September.

**Quit rates.** During the experiment, quit rates by assigned group were 7.20% in control and 4.80% in treatment, with P = 0.043 for the difference. These are experiment-period rates, not annual rates. The 2.40-percentage-point difference is about a 33% reduction relative to control.

The experiment was nominally six months long. It effectively ended on 21 January 2022, when a COVID case led the company to allow everyone to work from home. The attrition figure caption reports follow-up through 23 January. The study did not involve two years of randomized policy exposure.

**Take-up.** Among employees assigned eligibility, actual take-up was about 55% for volunteers and 40% for non-volunteers. These figures count Wednesday and Friday opportunities and exclude public holidays, personal holidays and excused absences. The denominator is therefore neither all employees nor all weekdays. Typical use was about one day weekly.

The quit-rate comparison therefore estimates the effect of being assigned to the optional policy. It does not estimate the effect of actually working from home twice every week.

## Implication for our review

My judgment is that this evidence warrants considering a comparable optional-policy trial for graduate office roles, subject to checking how similar our work and commuting conditions are. This is a policy judgment. The research does not show that another employer will achieve the same quit-rate reduction, or that fully remote work is equivalent.

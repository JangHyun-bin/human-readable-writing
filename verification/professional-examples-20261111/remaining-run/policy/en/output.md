# Evidence request on New York congestion pricing

To the transport-policy reviewer

## Reported results and missing definitions

Page 13 of MTA's December 17, 2025 board presentation, *2025 in Data*, reports three results. The tables below set out each claim, what it does not establish, and what the slide leaves unspecified.

| Reported result | What it does not establish | Unspecified on the slide |
|---|---|---|
| 11% fewer vehicle entries into the central business district (CBD), and 71,000 fewer per day | These are entry counts, not unique cars or vehicle-miles. The rounded figures do not establish an exact baseline count. | Observation and comparison periods; baseline construction |
| Car speeds within the CBD up 4.3% | A 4.3% speed increase does not mean travel time fell 4.3%. | Absolute speeds; weighting; hours covered; measurement provider; baseline; observation and comparison periods |
| PM2.5 down 22% in the first six months, crediting Cornell University, 2025 | The concentration claim does not establish a 22% reduction in emissions, all pollutants, or individual exposure. It cannot be generalized citywide. | Exact study dates; the concentration statistic; the percentage denominator; detailed geographic coverage |

MTA places the PM2.5 claim under a Congestion Relief Zone heading. The presentation title, *2025 in Data*, does not establish a common full-year comparison for these metrics.

## Evidence requested

I treat the implementing agency's summary as a record of attributed claims, not independent causal proof of the policy's effects. For independent review, I request:

- the traffic series and calculation methods;
- the missing periods and baselines;
- the underlying air-quality study's geography, statistic, counterfactual method, and uncertainty.

This is my request for evidence, not MTA's recommendation or a recommendation to repeal or expand congestion pricing.

# Trip.com retention evidence for hybrid-policy review

## Evidence for the HR director

Bloom, Han and Liang's [2024 Nature study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07500-2) covered 1,612 university-graduate engineering, marketing and finance employees in Trip.com's Airfare and IT divisions in Shanghai. Interns and new employees on probation were excluded. At this single employer, random assignment by odd or even birthday dates put 792 odd-birthday employees in a group allowed to work from home on Wednesday and Friday, with three office days, and 820 even-birthday employees in a five-office-day control group. The two home days were optional. The 518 initial volunteers started in the week of 9 August 2021, and the remaining 1,094 non-volunteers in the week of 13 September.

During the experiment, quit rates by assigned group were 7.20% in control and 4.80% in treatment, with P = 0.043 for the difference. These are experiment-period rates, not annual rates. The 2.40-percentage-point difference is about a 33% reduction relative to control. The nominal six-month experiment effectively ended on 21 January 2022 when a COVID case led the company to allow everyone to work from home, although the attrition figure caption reports follow-up through 23 January. This was not two years of randomized policy exposure.

Among employees assigned eligibility, actual take-up was about 55% for volunteers and 40% for non-volunteers across Wednesday and Friday opportunities, excluding public holidays, personal holidays and excused absences. These percentages use neither all employees nor all weekdays as their denominator. Typical use was about one day weekly. The quit-rate comparison therefore estimates assignment to the optional policy, not the effect of actually working from home twice every week.

## Implication for our review

My judgment is that this evidence warrants considering a comparable optional-policy trial for graduate office roles, subject to checking how similar our work and commuting conditions are. That is a policy judgment, not a research finding that another employer will achieve the same quit-rate reduction or that fully remote work is equivalent.
